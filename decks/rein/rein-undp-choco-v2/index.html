Who We Are

REIN Hubs: Renewable Energy Innovation Network

REIN Hubs is a partnership between PVBLIC Foundation and CleantechHUB that operates as a venture builder for climate solutions across five countries.

The REIN Partnership

PVBLIC Foundation brings institutional relationships with the UN system, the GISD Alliance (Global Investors for Sustainable Development), and multilateral development banks. Their expertise in SDG financing, blended finance structuring, and public-private partnerships provides the institutional architecture.

Fundación CleantechHUB (NIT [redacted]) has been operating in Colombia since 2020. CleantechHUB runs ClimateLaunchpad Colombia — the world’s largest green business ideas competition — and has built the Energía Coop platform, a digital toolkit for deploying community energy cooperatives. Across the region, the organisation has supported 222 startups and helped raise over $23 million in climate capital.

Together, REIN Hubs combines CleantechHUB’s on-the-ground venture-building methodology with PVBLIC’s institutional access and financing expertise. The result is a platform that can take a community energy concept from cooperative formation through to bankable project.

Strategic Context

Why Chocó, Why Now

Colombia’s policy environment has never been more aligned for community energy. The incoming administration inherits a set of regulatory tools and political commitments that make Chocó the ideal proof-of-concept for a national model.

📋

Plan Nacional de Desarrollo (PND)

The PND prioritises productive transformation in conflict-affected and ethnic territories, with explicit energy access targets for the Pacific and Amazonian regions.

🕊

PDET Post-Conflict Programme

Nine of Chocó’s municipalities are in the PDET territorial transformation programme — giving them priority access to public investment and institutional support.

⚡

CREG 101,072/2025

Colombia’s new energy communities regulation creates, for the first time, a legal framework for the cooperative structures this strategy proposes. The regulatory gap that blocked previous efforts is closing.

🌍

Just Energy Transition

Colombia’s commitment to a Just Energy Transition explicitly includes territorial equity and energy sovereignty for historically marginalised communities.

🏛

CONPES & DNP Strategy

CONPES documents on energy transition and DNP’s strategy for productive transformation provide the policy backbone for deploying community-owned infrastructure in underserved territories.

🇺🇳

UN Resident Coordinator’s Office

The RCO has identified energy poverty in conflict-affected territories as a national priority. Their coordination role across the UN system in Colombia provides institutional backing for territorial interventions.

The incoming administration will inherit the strongest regulatory and institutional alignment for community energy that Colombia has ever had. What is missing is not policy — it is a deployment mechanism that converts these commitments into working infrastructure in the territories that need it most. This strategy provides that mechanism, with Chocó as the proof-of-concept.

The Challenge

Two Energy Realities, One Country

Colombia generates 70% of its electricity from renewables. But 1.33 million households — spread across half the country’s territory — receive less than 8 hours of power per day. These are two different countries sharing one flag.

What Are ZNI?

Zonas No Interconectadas (Non-Interconnected Zones) are areas of Colombia that are not connected to the national electricity grid. They represent 52% of the country’s territory but receive only 7% of energy investment. These communities rely on expensive, unreliable diesel generators — when they have power at all.

Most ZNI localities are in remote Pacific and Amazonian regions, home to Afro-Colombian and Indigenous communities who hold collective land rights. Building transmission lines to reach them is economically unviable. The solution must be distributed: self-contained microgrids powered by solar and small hydro, located where the communities are.

The Investment Gap

Colombia’s renewable energy investment is almost entirely concentrated in grid-connected projects: large wind farms in La Guajira, utility-scale solar in César and Santander. These attract international capital because they are big enough to justify the transaction costs.

The 93% vs. 7% investment split between grid-connected and distributed energy is not an oversight — it is a structural failure. There is no mechanism that makes small community projects visible to institutional investors. A $500K microgrid in Quibdó costs the same to structure as a $500M wind farm in La Guajira, but returns a fraction of the fee. No bank will do the work.

Why Chocó

Chocó is Colombia’s most energy-poor department, and the place where every barrier to energy access converges. If a model can work here, it can work anywhere in the country.

Root Causes

Six Barriers That Keep Communities in the Dark

Understanding why the problem persists is the first step toward solving it. Each barrier reinforces the others, creating a system that no single intervention can break.

💰

1. Transaction Cost Mismatch

Structuring a $500K community project costs the same in legal, financial, and technical due diligence as a $500M utility-scale project. No bank will absorb those costs for the returns a microgrid generates. The economics of project finance are built for scale that these communities will never reach individually.

📜

2. Regulatory Fragmentation

Colombia’s energy regulation was designed for the national grid. ZNI communities must navigate CREG 174, CREG 091, Ley 1715, and Decreto 2236 — overlapping frameworks that were never harmonised for distributed energy. CREG 101,072/2025 begins to address this, but implementation lags.

🔌

3. No Grid to Connect To

These are not grid-extension problems. There is no grid within economic reach. Every installation must be a self-contained microgrid: generation, storage, and distribution. This requires different engineering, different business models, and different risk profiles than grid-connected solar.

🤝

4. Consulta Previa Complexity

82% of Chocó is ethnic territory. Colombian constitutional law (and ILO Convention 169) requires consulta previa — free, prior, and informed consent — for any project on collective lands. This is not a bureaucratic formality; it is a right. But it requires cultural competency, long timelines, and genuine partnership.

🏢

5. Weak Local Institutions

IPSE (the government agency for ZNI energy solutions) has limited execution capacity and budget. There are no local EPC contractors with microgrid experience in Chocó. Municipal governments lack the technical staff to evaluate proposals. The institutional infrastructure for energy deployment simply does not exist.

📈

6. No Aggregation Vehicle

This is the core gap. Individual projects are too small for institutional capital. There is no mechanism that bundles community-scale energy projects into a portfolio that pension funds, development banks, or green bond buyers can underwrite. That is precisely what this strategy addresses.

Learning from Precedent

What Hasn’t Worked — and Why

Colombia is not starting from zero. Several approaches have been tried. Understanding why they fell short clarifies what a successful model must do differently.

  1. Colombia’s Minigranja Programme

    Created 151 registered small-scale solar installations across ZNI regions. But each installation was structured as a one-off project with bespoke legal, technical, and financial arrangements. No standardised structure meant no ability to aggregate. Each project reinvented the wheel, and the programme could not scale beyond donor-funded deployments.

  2. IPSE Direct Implementation

    The Institute for Planning and Promotion of Energy Solutions (IPSE) is the government agency responsible for ZNI energy. It has built solar installations and diesel-hybrid systems, but with limited budget and execution capacity. IPSE operates as a project implementer, not a market builder. It cannot attract private capital at the scale required.

  3. La Guajira Wind Projects

    Colombia’s largest renewable energy initiative attracted international attention and billions in committed investment. But the consulta previa process with Wayuu communities has taken over 2,000 days in some cases, with projects still not operational. The lesson is clear: trust cannot be shortcut. Community engagement is not a box to check — it is the foundation of any project on collective land.

  4. International DFI Financing

    The IDB, World Bank, and EIB have deployed $100M+ per project into grid-connected renewable energy in Colombia. But none will touch a $500K community microgrid. The overhead of due diligence, environmental and social safeguards, and portfolio management simply does not justify the ticket size. DFI capital flows to where the infrastructure already exists.

  5. The Gap

    What is missing is not money, technology, or political will. It is a mechanism that bundles small community projects into something institutional investors can underwrite. A standardised deployment system that handles cooperative formation, training, financial structuring, and operation — all in a replicable package. That is what Energía Coop provides.

The Solution

Energía Coop: Energy Cooperative in a Box

CleantechHUB has built energiacoop.cleantechhub.net — a complete deployment system for community-owned renewable energy cooperatives, adapted for Colombia’s regulatory framework and inspired by European models that have produced 438 energy cooperatives in the Netherlands alone.

The solution is not more one-off projects. It is a standardised deployment system that takes communities from initial diagnosis through cooperative formation, project development, and ongoing operation — bundling small projects into a portfolio large enough for institutional capital, with communities owning 30% of every project they host.

Four-Phase Deployment Process

🔍

1. Diagnóstico

2–4 Weeks

Community assessment, solar resource mapping, governance capacity evaluation, consulta previa scoping. Determines readiness and identifies the productive uses that will generate revenue.

📚

2. Formación

8–12 Weeks

60-hour cooperative curriculum covering governance, financial management, technical operations, and regulatory compliance. Communities learn to own and operate their energy systems.

🏗

3. Proyecto

12–20 Weeks

Cooperative legal formation, financial structuring, equipment procurement, installation, and grid commissioning. Each project follows a standardised template enabling portfolio aggregation.

⚙

4. Operación

Ongoing

Community-managed operations with remote monitoring, scheduled maintenance, revenue collection, and progressive ownership transfer. Supported by the Energía Coop digital tools and knowledge base.

European Lessons, Colombian Adaptation

The Energía Coop model draws on proven European cooperative energy systems, adapted for Colombia’s unique regulatory and social context:

🇩🇰 Denmark: Mandatory Community Participation

Danish law requires 20% community ownership in all wind and solar projects. This principle is embedded in the Energía Coop model, with 30% community equity as the floor.

🇳🇱 Netherlands: Development Fund Model

Energie Samen (Netherlands) uses a pre-project development fund to de-risk community projects before they seek institutional capital. 438 cooperatives have been formed using this approach.

🇩🇪 Germany: Second-Tier Cooperative

Bürgerwerke’s model of a second-tier cooperative enables joint commercialisation of energy across many small cooperatives — the aggregation principle at the heart of this strategy.

🇨🇴 Colombia: Micro-Investment Access

Community Participation Certificates from COP 200,000 (~$50 USD) allow even the lowest-income households to become cooperative members and equity holders.

Cooperative Ownership Structure

Each community energy project uses a patrimonio autónomo (autonomous trust) structure, with dual-class participation certificates:

Class A — Community Certificates (30%)

Held collectively by the community cooperative. Cannot be sold to external parties. Carry voting rights on operational decisions. Valued through non-cash equity: land access, construction labour, social license, and local data collection.

Class B — Investor Certificates (70%)

Held by the portfolio vehicle. Carry preferential financial returns. Gradually convert to community ownership over 15–20 years as assets depreciate and community capacity grows.

Financial Instruments

The model leverages Colombia’s existing incentive framework:

Ley 1715 Tax Incentives

Income tax deduction (50% of investment), accelerated depreciation, VAT exclusion, and customs duty exemption for renewable energy equipment.

FENOGE Co-Financing

The national energy fund (Fondo de Energías No Convencionales y Gestión Eficiente de la Energía) provides up to 60% project co-financing for community-scale installations in ZNI territories.

Why this matters: In Chocó’s ethnic territories, land is collectively held under Colombian constitutional protections (Art. 63, 329). Individual ownership models conflict with Afro-Colombian and Indigenous communal governance. The patrimonio autónomo structure bridges Colombian financial law with collective rights — giving communities real equity in an institutional-grade legal structure, compliant with Decreto 2236 and CREG 101,072/2025.

Revenue Streams

Microgrids generate revenue from multiple sources, reducing dependence on any single income stream and improving the risk profile for investors.

Tariff Collection

Regulated electricity tariffs paid by households and businesses. Even at below-national-average rates, solar microgrids cost less than diesel.

Carbon Credits

Each kWh of solar replacing diesel generates verifiable emission reductions. Bundled across the portfolio, these are tradeable at voluntary market rates.

Productive Use Surplus

Cold chain for fisheries, water purification, and agricultural processing create economic value that raises ability — and willingness — to pay.

Data Services

Grid monitoring and usage data has commercial value for energy planning, climate modelling, and ESG reporting by institutional investors.

Decision Gates

Gate 1 — Month 4

Territorial Readiness

At least 10 of 15 target municipalities assessed. Consulta previa scoping confirms community willingness in at least 8 municipalities. If fewer than 8 engage, pilot geography is restructured.

Gate 2 — Month 8

Financial Vehicle Validated

Portfolio SPV legal structure completed. At least one GISD Alliance member issues a Letter of Intent. CAF confirms A/B loan applicability. If no institutional investor signals intent, the financial architecture requires revision.

Gate 3 — Month 10

Pipeline Quality

At least 40 of 60 candidate projects score bankable under the standardised scoring methodology. Each bankable project has a complete profile for independent investor review.

Gate 4 — Month 12

Investment Thesis Complete

Full investment thesis documented. Capital stack, risk allocation, return projections, and governance structure finalised. If all prior gates are met, the vehicle is ready for capitalisation.

Kill Criteria

If fewer than 30 projects score bankable, or if no institutional investor issues a Letter of Intent by month 10, the catalytic phase does not proceed. The design phase will have produced valuable learning — a territorial assessment, a community engagement methodology, and a financial model — but the vehicle is not ready for capitalisation.
